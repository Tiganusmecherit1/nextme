# What blood sugar is, in plain words

**Blood sugar** (blood glucose) means the amount of **glucose** (sugar) in your blood. Glucose is the brain's preferred fuel and an important fuel for muscles.

## The surprise: there is very little sugar in the blood

An adult has about 5 litres of blood. Dissolved in it are only **about 4–5 grams of glucose**, roughly **one teaspoon**. That is all. Yet a single can of soft drink has 7 times more sugar.

This means that every time you eat carbohydrates, the body has to **quickly move** glucose out of the blood and into cells so levels do not rise too much. And between meals it has to **release** glucose from its reserves so levels do not fall too low.

## Who keeps blood sugar in balance

- **The pancreas** – the "thermostat". It makes **insulin** (lowers blood sugar) and **glucagon** (raises it).
- **The liver** – the "reservoir". It stores glycogen and releases glucose into the blood between meals and at night. It can also make new glucose.
- **The muscles** – the "biggest consumer". After a meal, muscles take up most of the glucose.
- **The brain** – a constant consumer, ~120 g a day.
- **The gut** – produces hormones (such as GLP-1) that prepare the pancreas before sugar reaches the blood.

## Normal values

In Romania, blood sugar is usually measured in **mg/dL**:

| Situation | Value (mg/dL) |
|---|---|
| Normal, in the morning, fasting | 70–99 |
| Normal, 2 hours after a meal | below 140 |
| Prediabetes, fasting (ADA) | 100–125 |
| Diabetes, fasting (on two measurements) | 126 or higher |

(Other countries use mmol/L: divide the mg/dL value by 18. For example, 90 mg/dL ≈ 5 mmol/L.)

## Swings are normal

Blood sugar **rises after a meal** in everyone. In a healthy person it usually climbs to about 120–140 mg/dL and returns to normal within 2–3 hours. The problem is not that it rises, but:

- **how high** it goes,
- **how quickly** it comes back down,
- **how high** your morning fasting level is.

> [!NOTE]
> **Did you know** blood sugar can rise **without food**? Stress, lack of sleep, an infection or even a very intense workout release hormones (adrenaline, cortisol) that make the liver release glucose.

> [!CAUTION]
> If you are very thirsty, urinate often, lose weight for no reason or have blurred vision, see a doctor and get your blood sugar checked. These can be signs of diabetes.

## In short

- There are only ~4–5 g of glucose in the blood, about a teaspoon.
- The pancreas, liver and muscles keep blood sugar between ~70 and ~140 mg/dL.
- A rise after meals is normal; what matters is how high it goes and how fast it comes back.
