# Insulin: the key that opens your cells

**Insulin** is a hormone made by the **beta cells** of the pancreas. Its main job: to tell cells "there is food in the blood, take it".

## How it works, by comparison

Imagine muscle and fat cells as houses with locked doors. Glucose from the blood is waiting outside, in the street.

1. You eat. Glucose from the food enters the blood.
2. The pancreas senses the rise and releases **insulin**.
3. Insulin attaches to the cells, like a **key in a lock**.
4. The cells bring special glucose "doors" (called **GLUT4**) to their surface.
5. Glucose goes in, and blood sugar falls back to normal.

## What else insulin does

Insulin is a **storage hormone**:

- it helps muscles and liver make **glycogen** (stored sugar),
- it helps muscles take up **amino acids** and build proteins,
- it tells fat cells to **store** fat and not release it.

That is why fat burning drops temporarily when insulin is high. When insulin is low (between meals, at night, during exercise), fat leaves its stores more easily.

> [!WARNING]
> **Myth vs reality:** "insulin makes you fat, so every carbohydrate makes you fat". Insulin rises after every meal, including after protein, and then falls again. Over 24 hours, what decides whether fat builds up is your **total energy balance**. The real problem is **chronically high** insulin from insulin resistance (next lesson).

## Muscle: the sugar sponge

Skeletal muscles take up most (about **80%**) of the glucose that leaves the blood under the action of insulin. The **more muscle** you have and the **more active** it is, the better you control your blood sugar.

And movement has a superpower: **working muscle can take up glucose even without insulin**. Muscle contraction itself brings the GLUT4 "doors" to the surface. That is why a walk after a meal lowers blood sugar even in people with diabetes.

## Diabetes, in brief

- **Type 1:** the immune system destroys the beta cells. The body no longer makes insulin. Treatment is insulin, for life.
- **Type 2:** cells respond poorly to insulin (resistance), and over time the pancreas cannot keep up. It is the most common type (over 90% of cases) and is closely linked to lifestyle, although genetics matter too.

> [!CAUTION]
> If you have diabetes and take insulin or sulfonylureas, exercise and diet changes can lower your blood sugar too much (hypoglycaemia). Talk to your diabetes doctor before changing anything.

## In short

- Insulin is the key that lets glucose into cells.
- It is a storage hormone: glycogen, protein, fat.
- Muscle is the biggest glucose consumer and, when it works, takes up glucose even without insulin.
