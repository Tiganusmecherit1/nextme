# Glycaemic index and glycaemic load

## Glycaemic index (GI)

The glycaemic index shows **how quickly and how much** blood sugar rises after a food, compared with pure glucose (which has GI = 100).

- **Low GI:** 55 or less,
- **Medium GI:** 56–69,
- **High GI:** 70 or more.

| Food | GI (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Lentils, beans, chickpeas | 25–40 |
| Apple, orange | 35–45 |
| Oats (large flakes) | ~55 |
| Pasta cooked al dente | 45–55 |
| Real wholemeal bread | 50–70 |
| White rice | 65–75 |
| Polenta (mămăligă) | ~65–70 |
| White bread | ~75 |
| Boiled / mashed potatoes | 75–85 |
| Cornflakes | ~80 |

(Values vary a lot with variety, cooking and measurement method; they are approximate.)

## The problem with the glycaemic index

GI is measured for a portion containing **50 g of carbohydrates**. But in real life you do not always eat 50 g. Watermelon has a high GI (~70–75), but one slice has few carbohydrates, so the real effect is small.

## Glycaemic load (GL)

Glycaemic load also accounts for **how much** you eat:

**GL = GI × grams of carbohydrates in the portion ÷ 100**

- **Low GL:** 10 or less,
- **Medium GL:** 11–19,
- **High GL:** 20 or more.

Example: a slice of watermelon (120 g, ~6 g carbohydrates, GI 72) has GL ≈ 4, which is low. A large plate of white rice (~60 g carbohydrates, GI 70) has GL ≈ 42, which is high.

## What changes the glucose response

The same food can have different effects depending on:

- **Cooking:** pasta al dente has a lower GI than overcooked pasta. Potatoes and rice that are **cooled** after cooking form **resistant starch**, which is digested more slowly.
- **What you eat with it:** protein, fat, fibre and vinegar slow absorption.
- **Order:** vegetables and protein before carbohydrates reduce the spike (next lesson).
- **The person:** an Israeli study (Zeevi, 2015) continuously monitored the blood sugar of 800 people and found **large differences between people** for the same food, partly linked to the microbiome.

> [!WARNING]
> **Myth vs reality:** "low GI = healthy". Chocolate and crisps have a relatively low GI because of their fat, but they are not good choices. Use GI as a tool, not as the only criterion.

> [!TIP]
> **In practice:** you do not need to memorise tables. Remember the rule: **the less processed a carbohydrate is, the more fibre it has, and the more you eat it alongside protein and vegetables, the more gently your blood sugar rises.**

## In short

- GI shows how quickly blood sugar rises; GL also accounts for the portion.
- Cooking, combinations and food order change the response.
- People react differently; use GI as a guide, not a law.
