# The gut–brain axis: the "second brain"

Have you ever felt "butterflies in your stomach" before an exam? Or had diarrhoea before an important event? It is not a coincidence. **The gut and the brain talk constantly.**

## The second brain

The gut wall contains the **enteric nervous system**: a network of **hundreds of millions of neurons** (comparable to the spinal cord). It can coordinate digestion almost on its own, without orders from the brain. That is why it is sometimes called "the second brain".

## How they communicate

1. **The vagus nerve** – a long "cable" linking the brain to the gut. Most signals travel **from the gut to the brain**, not the other way round.
2. **Gut hormones** – GLP-1, PYY, cholecystokinin, ghrelin. They tell the brain when you are hungry and when you are full.
3. **The immune system** – inflammation in the gut can send signals throughout the body.
4. **Substances made by bacteria** – short-chain fatty acids and other molecules can influence nerves and hormones.

## Serotonin from the gut

About **90% of the body's serotonin** is made in the gut, by special cells in the gut wall, and some bacteria stimulate this production (Yano, 2015).

But note: **gut serotonin does not reach the brain**. In the gut it controls intestinal movement and other functions. So "the gut makes the happiness hormone" is an oversimplification.

## What we know about stress, anxiety and the microbiome

- Animal studies show the microbiome can influence behaviour and the stress response.
- In humans, there are **associations** between the microbiome and depression or anxiety, but it is hard to tell cause from effect.
- Some small studies of probiotics ("psychobiotics") have shown modest effects on mood, but the results are not yet solid enough.

> [!WARNING]
> **Myth vs reality:** "a probiotic will cure your depression". There is not enough evidence. Depression and anxiety are serious medical conditions that need specialist assessment and treatment.

> [!CAUTION]
> If you have felt sad, hopeless or anxious for more than two weeks, talk to your GP or a psychologist. You are not alone and help is available.

> [!TIP]
> **In practice:** what is good for the gut is good for the brain: fibre, varied plants, fermented foods, movement, sleep and less stress. And the reverse: a calm walk after a meal helps both digestion and the mind.

## In short

- The gut has hundreds of millions of neurons and talks to the brain through the vagus nerve and hormones.
- 90% of serotonin is made in the gut, but it does not reach the brain.
- The microbiome–mood link is real but still being studied; it does not replace treatment.
