# What happens in your bones when you lift weights

You may have heard of **Wolff's law**, put forward more than 100 years ago by a German surgeon: **bone adapts to the forces acting on it**. Today we also know how, at the level of the cell.

## Step by step, in a squat

**1. The bone bends, microscopically.** When you go down with a bar on your shoulders, your femur, tibia and spine bear forces several times your body weight. The bone deforms by fractions of a percent, invisibly but really. The same happens when tendons pull: strong muscles tug on the bone.

**2. The fluid in the bone moves.** The deformation pushes fluid through the network of microscopic channels where the **osteocytes** live. It is like water flowing through a squeezed sponge.

**3. Osteocytes "feel" the flow.** They have fine extensions that detect fluid movement and deformation. This is **mechanotransduction**: mechanical force becomes a chemical signal.

**4. Osteocytes give the order.** At rest, osteocytes produce a substance called **sclerostin**, which **brakes** the builders (osteoblasts). When bone is loaded, osteocytes **reduce sclerostin**, and the brake comes off. At the same time, they send signals that can limit demolition.

**5. The builders get to work.** Osteoblasts lay down new bone exactly **where the forces were greatest**. Bone becomes thicker, denser and has an internal structure oriented along the direction of the effort.

**6. It takes time.** A remodelling cycle takes **months**. That is why the effects on bone density show up after **6–12 months** of consistent training.

## What kind of loading works

Bone studies show the best signal is:

- **large** (relatively heavy weights or impact),
- **fast** (jumps, dynamic movements),
- **varied** (different directions, not always the same movement),
- **short and repeated** (bone "desensitises" after a few dozen reps; several short sessions are better than one very long one).

**Swimming and cycling**, although excellent for the heart, put **little load** on bones. Walking helps a little. Strength training and jumping help the most.

## The LIFTMOR study

For a long time it was thought that women with fragile bones should not lift heavy weights. The Australian **LIFTMOR** study (Watson, 2018) tested exactly this: postmenopausal women with **osteopenia or osteoporosis** did **30 minutes of training twice a week for 8 months**: deadlifts, squats, overhead presses (at ~80–85% of maximum, after a learning period) and jumps, **under close supervision**.

Results:

- **bone density at the spine and femoral neck increased**, while in the light-exercise group it decreased,
- **strength, posture and balance** improved,
- there were no fractures or serious injuries linked to the programme.

> [!CAUTION]
> If you have **diagnosed osteoporosis** or have had fractures, do not start a heavy programme on your own. LIFTMOR was done **under the supervision** of specialists, with gradual progression. Ask your doctor for a recommendation and, ideally, work with a qualified physiotherapist or coach.

> [!NOTE]
> **Did you know** tennis players have visibly thicker, denser bones in their playing arm than in the other one? It is one of the clearest demonstrations that bone responds **locally** to forces.

> [!TIP]
> **In practice:** 2–3 full-body strength sessions a week, plus a few light jumps (if your joints and doctor allow), plus protein, calcium and vitamin D. That is the "recipe" for bones.

## In short

- Weights bend bone microscopically, fluid moves, osteocytes sense it.
- Osteocytes reduce sclerostin, and osteoblasts build new bone where it is needed.
- Strength and impact work even in women with osteopenia, if progressive and supervised.
