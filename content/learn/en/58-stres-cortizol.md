# Stress and cortisol: the body's alarm

Imagine one of our ancestors seeing a bear. Within seconds, his body:

- releases **adrenaline**: the heart beats faster, breathing speeds up, muscles get more blood,
- releases **cortisol**: the liver pushes glucose into the blood so there is fuel to fight or flee,
- pauses digestion, reproduction and long-term repair.

This is the **stress response**, and it has kept us alive for hundreds of thousands of years.

## Cortisol is a good hormone

- It **wakes you up** in the morning: it has a natural peak in the first 30–45 minutes after waking.
- It **mobilises energy** when you need it.
- It controls **inflammation**.
- It helps regulate **blood pressure**.

Without cortisol (for example, in Addison's disease), people cannot survive without treatment.

## The problem: the alarm that never switches off

Today's bear is a boss, a bill, traffic, phone notifications, worries. The stress response is the same, but it **never switches off**. Chronic stress can lead to:

- **poor sleep**, which in turn increases stress (a vicious circle),
- **cravings for food high in sugar and fat**: in one study (Epel, 2001), women who responded to stress with higher cortisol ate more sweets after the stressful task,
- **higher blood sugar** and, over time, more **abdominal fat**,
- higher blood pressure,
- poorer recovery after training,
- irritability, anxiety, fatigue.

## Training: good stress

Training is also a **stress**: it temporarily raises cortisol and adrenaline. The difference is that it is **short**, followed by **recovery**, and the body adapts by becoming stronger. Researchers call this **hormesis**: a small dose of stress makes you stronger.

But if you add very hard training on top of an already very stressful life, with little sleep and little food, you can end up exhausted.

> [!WARNING]
> **Myth vs reality:** "cortisol makes you fat, so you have to lower it with supplements". Cortisol itself does not "make" fat out of nothing; chronic stress affects sleep, hunger and food choices. "Anti-cortisol" supplements have weak evidence. Sleep, movement, relationships and stress management have much better evidence.

## What works for stress

- **Movement**, even a 10–20 minute walk.
- **Slow breathing**: breathe in for 4 seconds, out for 6 seconds, for 3–5 minutes. A long out-breath activates the vagus nerve and the "rest" system.
- **Nature**: time in a park, forest, the mountains.
- **Relationships**: a phone call to a friend.
- **Consistent sleep.**
- **Boundaries**: notifications off, screen-free time.

> [!CAUTION]
> If stress has been affecting your sleep, work or relationships for more than a few weeks, or if you have panic attacks or dark thoughts, talk to your GP or a psychologist. Mental health is as important as physical health.

> [!TIP]
> **In practice:** set a 3-minute timer and breathe in for 4 seconds, out for 6 seconds. Try it before bed or when you feel you are "boiling over".

## In short

- Cortisol is a vital hormone; the problem is chronic stress that never switches off.
- Chronic stress disrupts sleep and raises sugar cravings and blood sugar.
- Movement, slow breathing, nature, relationships and sleep are the best "antidotes".
